# 3. Three chairs

Someone provides the room: power, cooling, the building, the duty roster for the hall. Someone operates the equipment that was put in the room: servers, the network they own, the spares, the tickets. Someone is the customer who specified the equipment and may run the work on it. Three chairs. A due diligence that merges them will sign one party's pass for another's risk.

Write who delivers what, by function, not by brand. The room can be excellent and the equipment count wrong. The equipment can be counted and the tenant's test unwitnessed. The tenant can have a pass document and the operator can have no spare. Each of those is a separate sentence.

Ask, for each contracted performance, which chair owes it. A service level that the end customer cares about may depend on the room provider's power, the operator's spare, and the customer's own acceptance. If you cannot name the chair, you cannot say the level is achievable. You can say the papers do not show who owes it. That is a finding.

Do not take a market story from one chair and attach it to the plant. Fitness for a later tenant, a change of use, a retail shape instead of a lease, is a commercial scenario. It is not evidenced by a walk. If the engagement requires you to note it, label it a scenario. Scenarios do not get the verb "can."

## Decisions to record

- Three chairs, by function. No brands required.
- For each contracted performance: which chair owes it, or "not shown."
- A reuse or exit story labeled as a scenario, or not written.
