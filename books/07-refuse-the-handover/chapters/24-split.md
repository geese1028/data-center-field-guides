# 24. Their signatures are not yours

Several people may sign on the same day. The contractor signs that they built what they were paid to build. The tester signs the short pack, for the scope they ran. The owner signs turnover for that scope. A vendor signs that their machine met a factory sheet. None of those signatures is the operator's sentence. You may watch them sign. You may not treat their ink as a substitute for yours, and you may not let your ink be the one that completes a set you have not read.

A signature block with four lines and one paragraph above it is a trap. The paragraph is usually the wide sentence. Each line thinks it is signing only its own hat. The reader who comes later sees four names under commissioned. If your name is one of the four, you signed the paragraph. Strike the paragraph, or use a separate page. Chapter 1. Do not rely on the title under your name to shrink the paragraph. Titles lose. The sentence wins.

## What you can point to

You can point to their signature as a fact: this person signed that page, on this date. You cannot point to it as proof of your gates. A contractor's signature does not create a file judgment. A vendor's factory signature does not accept the screen image on the floor. An owner's turnover does not mean you can operate. If the owner wants the hall called accepted, and you cannot sign that, the owner may still sign their own sentence. They may not sign yours. Chapter 13. Write the split in the record so a later reader does not merge the names.

If they refuse to split the page, and the only way to record their legitimate signature is to stand under the wide sentence, the page waits. Their delay is not your emergency. A commercial clock, chapter 1, can be started by the owner's signature on a page that does not include your sentence. If their form cannot do that, their form is wrong. You do not repair it with your name.

## Partial buildings

A building delivered in parts will produce a stack of narrow signatures that someone later staples into one wide one. Your sheet for this part names the part. It says the other parts were not in this meeting. When the next part comes, you write a new sheet. You do not initial the old one to cover the new part. The old limits are still true. The new part has its own file, its own points, its own punch. Stapling is how a suite that passed launders a suite that did not.

If they ask you to sign the common plant once, for all future suites, refuse the future. You may sign the common plant for the sentence the evidence supports today. A future suite that connects to it is a new meeting. Connection is not inheritance. The sixth book's rename rule is the right shape: a new path is a new source. A new suite is a new path until its own sheets exist.

## Decisions to record

- Other names on the day, and that none of them replaced your gates.
- Wide paragraph struck, or your name kept off that page.
- This part named. Future parts not included.
- A staple you did not make.
